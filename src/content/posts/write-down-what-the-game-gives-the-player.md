Every game starts with a vision: a few pillars, a pitch, perhaps a first design document. The hard part is everything that happens next, as that vision is translated into technical designs, production plans and thousands of individual decisions made by dozens of people. Somewhere along the way, it's very easy for a team to lose sight of what the game is actually supposed to be.

The most effective tool I've found for preventing that is also one of the simplest: write down, clearly and early, what the game is going to give its players.

## What Nosgoth taught me

I saw this most clearly on *Nosgoth*, an asymmetric multiplayer game in which vampires and human hunters fought with completely different abilities and ways of playing. Asymmetric games are hard to reason about. Each side needs to feel powerful, fair and fun in its own way, and the interactions between them are where most of the surprises live.

Defining the desired player experience through clear user stories meant everyone understood what the game was supposed to be, even at the paper design stage. Not "the vampire has a pounce ability", but what it should feel like to play as the vampire, and what it should feel like to be hunted by one.

Those stories then shaped everything that followed. They told us what to prototype first, what to look for in early hands-on playtests, and where the biggest risks were.

## A touchstone, not a document

The real value of user stories is that they don't go away. Once the project is in full production, they become a constant point of comparison: is the game we're building actually delivering the experience we promised ourselves?

That only works if a team is willing to look honestly. Regular content and project reviews, with unflinching appraisals of progress, are invaluable. I've seen how signing off on content too early, often with the best of intentions, simply stores up bigger problems for later. It's much cheaper to say "this isn't there yet" in month six than in month twenty.

## Set the standards at the start

User stories work best as part of a wider habit of establishing standards at the very beginning of a project. That means agreeing on:

- **Quality benchmarks:** which games, or which moments in games, are we measuring ourselves against?
- **Deliverable expectations:** what does "done" look like for a milestone?
- **Communication channels:** how will information get to the people who need it?
- **User stories:** what is the game going to give its players?

Get these agreed early and a team avoids many of the common pitfalls. Leave them vague and every disagreement later on has to be argued from first principles.

## Ambition has to be achievable

There's one more reason I value this approach. A clear statement of the player experience makes it much easier to tell what is essential to the game's vision and what isn't. Ambition is always laudable, but it has to be achievable within the time and budget available.

When a project is under pressure, and every project is at some point, the user stories are what let a team cut wisely. Keep what delivers the experience. Question everything else. In my experience, a focus on doing the essentials brilliantly almost always produces a better game than struggling to deliver across too many areas.

It's a lesson I've found applies well beyond video games. Running a tabletop campaign is, in its own way, a constant exercise in asking what each player came to the table for, and making sure the session gives it to them.

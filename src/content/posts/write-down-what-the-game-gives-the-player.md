Every game starts with a vision, and that can be communicted in any number of ways: an X-statement, a handful of design pillars, a pitch, a game vision, perhaps a draft game design document or even a rip-o-matic can all help get everyone excited and on the same page at the same time. That can be easy part, certainly compared to what comes next. 
The vision needs to be fully articulated and then dissected to help inform not only the full GDD but also all the other designs: technical, art and audio. And the less sexy ones too: the production plan,  schedule and roadmap. 

Then the team is ramping up and there are thousands of decisions to be made as the game takes shape, evolving from concept through prototypes, then first playable code and a vertical slice. Depending on the scale of the project, this can all be happening really fast or take months (or longer for the lucky few :)), and with all that noise and activity it can be all too easy for individuals, departments or even whole teams to lose sight of what the game they are working in is actually supposed to be. 

Everyone will have their own way to work around this problem, but for me one of the most effective solutions is also one of the simplest: User Stories. 

## What a User Story looks like.
It's a statement, a couple of lines or a paragraph to describe a part of your game, in terms of what it means for the end user. What do you want the player’s experience to be when they engage with this system or content. Use clear, unambiguous language, but don't be afraid of a few adjectives: this is about the hands-on emotional reaction you want to elicit as well as the gameplay loop you’re looking to promote. 
Write them early, and make sure everyone has read them and knows what you are all aiming for. Yes, there will be an element of subjectivity here, but that’s OK. These will be a touchstone for development, and you’ll have plenty of time to argue about whether or not you’ve delivered them as you move forward.  

There's a couple of actual examples coming up next. 


## A case study: *Nosgoth* 
I saw the value of this practice perhaps most clearly on *Nosgoth*, an ambitiously asymmetrical PvP game set in a previously unexplored period in the *Legacy of Kain* timeline, that was developed for Square Enix by the talented team at Psyonix. A defining feature for the project was that Vampires and the Humans hunting them had radically different playstyles: the Vampires were all about mobility, hit-and-run attacks, and ferocious melee attacks;  while the Humans were equipped with powerful weaponry and gadgets to let them take down their before they got a chance to even get into melee. If you had played any other 3rd-person shooter, you were going to feel at home with the Humans, but the Vampires were more original and more of a challenge, especially when you factored in their movement abilities – they could all climb any vertical surface, and one class could freely fly around the levels. The two sides needed to feel equally but differently powerful, which would require constant evaluation and balance tweaks, but before the team could get to that point, there was huge amount of work needed just to establish what the core experience should look like. 
As a small and agile team, Psyonix could prototype rapidly, and lots of different concepts were prototyped and tested, but there came a point where it was clear to everyone that we needed a better set of guidelines to help steer this process, and this is where the user stories came in.  We worked on defining what each class should *feel* like to play, and came up with statements like these:

*“As a Reaver I am the most acrobatic of the vampire classes, I can run faster and leap further. I hunt by stalking my prey from afar and using my pounce ability by crouching to ready the attack. I growl in anticipation and target my enemy. When I release my pounce I fly at the enemy at great speed and tackle them to the ground. I slash at them in a frenzy while they struggle to escape before finally leaping backwards off the enemy after having done my damage. I can also use my pounce as a super leap in any direction. My smoke ability allows me to create a great distraction in the battlefield, clouding the local area in darkness. I use this as a way of confusing the enemy, as an escape tactic, or a way of healing in the field due the dark shroud around me. I also have an evasion ability which gives my character the ability to dodge almost any ranged attack for a short period of time, but prevents me from attacking. I have a fast melee combo attack which I typically use a finishing move in close range.”*

*“As the Assassin, I am a trained vampire killer who excels at hunting and slaying the enemy. I carry a marvel of human ingenuity - the automatic crossbow - that fires bolts incredibly rapidly, sending a hail of death at my foes. No single bolt can kill, but the unrelenting assault can stop any vampire in its tracks at close or medium range. When I need to slow a fleeing vampire or stop one’s advance upon me or my allies, I carry a silver Bolas I can hurl. It ensnares the enemy’s feet and knocks them down long enough for me to flee or finish them off. I also carry a concoction I can fire from my crossbow that explodes on impact, damaging any vampires caught in the radius and setting them aflame. As a last resort, I carry a vicious machete I can use to slay vampires at close range.”*

Each of those is a summary of the user experience you’d have if you picked on of those classes, the tools at your disposal, the tactics you’d find effective. You can read it and easily imagine how the game will play even without touching a controller or mouse &keyboard.  

Could they be more emotion-heavy? Yes, on reflection I’d probably punch up ‘the feels’ a bit more.

Could they be more granular? Sure, and I’ve worked on projects where we broke content down to a level of detail that was almost unmanageable. And these were the first ones that were written, with later iterations changing form. 

Should we have done this earlier than we did? Yes, but better late than never, and the game that evolved after this – that was shaped by the user stories for each class, level, and game mode. The earlier you have them, the more useful they can be in informing the essential early prototyping, hands-on playtesting, and risk-mitigation stages.

Defining the player experience you’re looking for means that everyone can understand what the game *is*. Not “this class of Vampire has a pounce ability on RMB”, but what it should feel like to play as a Reaver: the build-up of anticipation, the explosive release of power, the frenzy of the takedown. And what it will be like for their prey, hearing that predatory growl, being slammed to the ground and somehow surviving the attack only to have the Vampire scurry off into the concealing shadows. 

## A touchstone, not a dead document
Plenty of designers can bemoan the fact that their lovably detailed design wiki isn’t being read by the right people.  The user stories are harder to avoid – we used them not only as touchstones for the day-to-day work but as part of the milestone definitions. From every gameplay session or brainstorming session to formal content review and payment sign-off, the user stories were there to act as a benchmark to help both developer and publisher evaluate and agree on whether the game was delivering the experience we had set out to deliver. 

As with any such tool, they only work if the team is willing to commit to ruthlessly honest self-appraisal. Regular content and project reviews, with unflinching appraisals of progress, are invaluable. I have sadly seen how prematurely signing off on content, often with the best intentions, will only lead to bigger problems in the future. 

User stories don’t need to be monolithic, if your game evolves in unforeseen directions for whatever reason. Just be really sure you’re changing them for the right reason: because it will make the game better, or (in a less ideal world) because other factors make it essential. Be open and honest about what you’re doing, and again you’ll avoid unpleasant surprises further down the line. 

## Ambition has to be achievable
A clear statement of the player experience makes it much easier to tell what is essential to the game's vision and what is not: while ambition is always laudable it must also be achievable.

When a project is under pressure (and every project is at some point), the user stories are what let a team make informed decisions about what to keep and what to cut. Prioritize what delivers the essential experience. Question anything else, even if it’s cool and fun. In my experience, a focus on doing the less brilliantly almost always produces a better game than ones which struggles to deliver across too many areas.

## Multimedia crossover potential
Given that this blog is also about TTRPGs, I also have to say it's a lesson that applies here too. Especially for new players, you need to clearly communicate what you as a GM are expecting to deliver in terms of tone, content, and acceptable behaviour. The difference here is that as one of those players, you get to shape those user stories as you discuss what you’re looking for, define Lines and Veils, and work with other players to build your new band of loveable rogues / knights in shining armour / wandering murder-hoboes. 

Or you could be, you know, heroic game developers.

I remember coming home from my first job in the games industry and explaining to my friends that I'd spent part of the day working out how much damage a sawed-off shotgun should do to a crowd of zombies. It was around then that I realised I had the coolest job in the world.

That was at Eurocom in the late 90's. I'd been hired as the company's first Assistant Producer, but I soon found myself taking on game design work, because that was where my real interest lay. My first project (after playing a lot of *Diablo* to see if would work on a PlayStation 1) was porting *Duke Nukem 3D* to the Nintendo 64. This turned out to be a crash course in first-person shooter design: how levels were built and triggered; how encounters flowed; how to pace a level and balance its difficulty. You learn so much about how something works, why it was done the way it was, when you have to rebuild it for a different machine, a different controller and a different audience. (You also learn a lot about what is acceptable for different platform holders, as you reskin strip club runways into a Dukeburger loading dock). 

## From Duke to Bond

Before long I was leading teams of twenty, then forty, people. We made an original third-person Duke Nukem game, *Zero Hour* (the one with the zombies in it), and then took on the next James Bond game, at a time when *GoldenEye* had just redefined what a console shooter could look like. Fortunately, I was too niave at the time to fully appreciate the pressure.

That game, *The World Is Not Enough*, was successful enough for EA to ask us for a follow-up that wasn't going to be tied to a new movie. We had free choice of which of the old films to base it on, and after lengthy deliberation decided on *You Only Live Twice*, before the decision came down that instead EA wanted it to be completely original, and so *Nightfire* was born (you can still see traces of the earlier YOLT design in the levels set in Japan). This shift meant collaborating with the lead writer from *The Sims*, and later the vehicle team from the *Need for Speed* team came on board to craft the racing levels. This all taught me early on how multi-team collaboration changes development, both in presenting new challenges but more importantly opening up amazing opportunities to learn from other experts - and of course to build a better game. 

Working on Bond also showed me what operating inside someone else's world really means. Licensed games come with franchise bibles, and you learn to respect the smallest of their fine print (as well as the unwritten rules: we were building a mission where the player, as Bond, had to defuse a bomb hidden in the toilets in the London Underground, and were told to move the device from the Ladies to the Gents since Bond apparently wouldn't go into the former). Working with such a famous licence comes with plenty of incredible and memorable moments, like meeting the producers of the Bond films, witnessing Denise Richards (but sadly not Pierce Brosnen) filming at Pinewood Studios, or how excited my level design team were to see photos of a scale model of one of the sets (Robbie Coltrane's cavier factory, in case you're curious) since it gave us great reference to work from. 

## Crossing the table

After nearly ten years in development I moved into publishing, first at Eidos and then at Square Enix, effectively working  as an external consultant with internal and external developers. My first task there was helping Pivotal Games transition the *Conflict* series from a tactical third-person game towards a more mass-market first-person shooter. At the same time I started working with teams in completely different genres to anything like I'd made before. That was when I realized that the same principles applied, regardless of the type of game or the size of the team.

Passion, vision, professionalism, and a real desire to bring fun, excitement and a sense of accomplishment to the player. That's what the great teams had in common, whether they were making a shooter, a ground-breaking superhero Metroidvania, an over-the-top open-world sandbox, or heart-warming (and -breaking) games about friendship, love, and loss in small town America.

I've been lucky enough to work with some truly talented developers during my career, on franchises as varied as *Life is Strange*, *Just Cause*, *Deus Ex*, *Tomb Raider*, *Legacy of Kain*, *Outriders*, and *Arkham Asylum*. I've also seen plenty of things not work out the way everyone planned at the start (a seemingly inescapable part of game development, sadly). Learning from those setbacks and mistakes, dealing with the unexpected, and coming back stronger is the real mark of a professional team.

## Why it still matters

Most of the last twenty years has been about observing, guiding, learning, and sharing what I've learned with the teams I work with. Much of that is about commercial realities: scope, schedules, budgets, what players will actually spend their time and money on. Everyone knows those things matter, but it is not always easy to keep sight of this when you're up close and personal with the daily work of actually making the game.

All of this effort has been in the pursuit of commercial success, of course, but also of fun. Of remembering the feeling I had on that early evening explaining zombie-related shotgun damage to my friends. Of taking that passion and filtering it through decades of practical experience to make better games, more efficiently, more predictably, for a whole new generation of gamers. 


That's what this site, and these posts, are about. I'll be musing about game design, about what I've learned from thirty years of making and publishing games, and about running tabletop games, which turns out to have a lot of overlap. I hope some of it is useful, or at least fun to read. Because in the end, fun is what matters.

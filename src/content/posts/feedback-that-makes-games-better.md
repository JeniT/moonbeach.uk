For nearly twenty years, a large part of my job has been reviewing other people's games and telling them what isn't working yet. I've done it from the publisher's side of the table at Eidos and Square Enix, with game directors, lead designers, creative directors, art directors and studio heads, on everything from single-page vision statements to near-final shipping builds.

It's a strange job when you describe it like that. Nobody gets into games hoping to be criticised. But I've come to think that good feedback is one of the most useful things anyone can bring to a project, and that bad feedback is one of the most damaging. The difference is rarely about whether the criticism is correct. It's about what the team does next.

## Leave the team wanting to fix it

The goal of feedback isn't to be right. It's to leave a team understanding the core issue and energised to solve it themselves. If a review ends with people feeling defensive or deflated, it has failed, however accurate it was.

In practice that means a few things:

- **Find the real problem.** Most notes are about symptoms. A level that "feels slow" might have a pacing problem, a navigation problem or a reward problem. The useful work is identifying which.
- **Explain why it matters.** Tie the issue back to the player's experience and to the game's own goals, not to personal taste.
- **Let the team own the solution.** I'm always happy to offer suggestions, and sometimes it's my job to make the final call. But a fix the team arrives at themselves is almost always better, and better understood, than one handed to them.

## Ask the difficult questions

Much of my role has been to encourage creators to ask themselves difficult questions. Is this content the best it can be? Does this work fully support the vision for the game? Are we working as smartly as we can? Why are we doing things this way?

These questions work best when they're asked regularly, in one-to-ones, content reviews and post-mortems, rather than saved up for a crisis. Asked often enough, they stop feeling like an audit and become simply how the team thinks.

## Use evidence wherever you can

I try to be open to input from any informed source, especially when it's supported by data or objective evidence. User research, focus testing, player telemetry and sentiment analysis all help to take the subjectivity out of a conversation. "I don't like this" is easy to argue with. "Most playtesters missed this objective" usually isn't.

Evidence also helps when opinions run high. Games are made by passionate people, and I've often found myself acting as a mediator between strongly held views. Data doesn't settle every argument, but it gives everyone something to stand on that isn't just conviction.

## Make the decision, and write it down

Having seen how badly prevarication can hurt a project and a team's morale, I'm a great believer in actually making decisions, recording them, and acting on them. The tactics are simple: get input from the critical stakeholders, lay out the alternatives and their likely outcomes, keep meetings on track, and give every action point an owner and a deadline. They sound obvious. They're surprisingly hard to do consistently.

Sometimes the decisions are difficult ones: a milestone that hasn't been met, a scope that needs cutting, occasionally a project that can't continue. Those calls are rarely welcome. But I've found that helping a team understand the reasoning behind them is an essential part of the job, and that it often leads to a better game. Doing the essentials brilliantly beats struggling to deliver everything.

## Make it safe to speak

Finally, feedback has to flow in every direction. I've always tried to build teams where every voice can be heard regardless of seniority or job title, and where people are accountable but also quick to credit each other. Some of the most useful insights I've had came from junior designers who challenged my own assumptions.

Above all, I love helping people make games. Feedback, done well, is just one of the ways of doing that.
